Pakistan’s Solar Revolution

What is driving rooftop solar, who benefits, who is left behind, and how the grid can evolve alongside it.

Pakistan is in the midst of a rooftop solar revolution. With high electricity prices and increasingly affordable solar technology, millions of households are turning to the sun to power their lives. But as solar adoption accelerates, it poses a big question: Can the national grid survive this transformation, or should it evolve alongside it?

What’s Fueling the Shift?

Between 2021 and 2024, electricity prices in Pakistan more than doubled, jumping from Rs. 25 to Rs. 54 per unit. This sharp increase made grid power unaffordable for many families. The announced national average tariff subsequently fell to Rs. 37.64 per unit in April 2025, but the incentive to reduce grid purchases remains. [14] At the same time, the cost of solar panels has fallen significantly, dropping by 61% over the three-year period described in 2024, from 28 cents to 11 cents per watt, with 2026 reporting placing panel prices around 10–12 cents per watt. [0][94] note

By 2024, global solar-panel and battery prices had reportedly fallen by over 90% since 2010, with further declines expected by 2030. [0]note This trend, combined with an oversupply of solar panels, especially from China, has made imports cheaper and accelerated Pakistan’s shift to rooftop solar.

In FY24 alone, Pakistan imported $2.1 billion worth of solar panels, triple the $714 million imported in 2023, and nearly seven times as much as the $310 million imported in 2020. The country is clearly investing heavily in solar as an alternative to expensive grid electricity. In FY25, module imports reached 17.9 GW; cumulative imports were about 55.7 GW by May 2026. [2][94] The country’s distributed solar fleet is estimated at around 38 GW in FY25. [2] note

Households that adopt rooftop solar can save substantially by using their own electricity instead of buying it from the grid. New users now operate under net billing, with imports charged at the retail tariff and exports compensated at the National Average Energy Purchase Price, around Rs. 10–11 per unit in the cited 2026 reports. Existing agreements received protection under subsequent changes. [35][97][98][106] Solar’s payback now depends more heavily on daytime self-consumption, installation cost and storage, making the household’s pattern of use central to the investment. [109] note

A Household Decision with a National Payoff

The savings reach beyond the household’s meter. CREA estimated that Pakistan’s solar expansion had avoided more than $12 billion in oil and gas imports by February 2026. Its analysis projected a further $6.3 billion by the end of 2026 if elevated fuel prices persisted. The latter is a conditional estimate, not a realised full-year saving. [113]

That makes rooftop generation a form of protection against international fuel shocks. It reduces the fuel Pakistan needs to buy when supplies tighten or prices rise. It cannot remove every outage or fuel shortage, but it gives households and businesses an additional source of power when the imported-energy system is under strain. [110][113]

Who’s Winning?

Affluent households that can afford rooftop solar are enjoying enormous benefits. They can gain greater energy independence, protect themselves from future price hikes and substantially reduce their electricity bills. For these consumers, solar isn’t just a power source; it’s freedom from the broken grid.

Who’s Being Left Behind?

For lower-income households, the upfront cost of solar remains out of reach, even as prices fall. Additionally, rooftop solar isn’t a viable option for those living in apartments or densely populated urban areas with limited roof space. As more well-off consumers leave the grid, the fixed costs of maintaining infrastructure are passed on to fewer users, raising prices for everyone else. This dynamic is widening Pakistan’s energy inequality.

Should the Government Fight the Solar Boom?

Cracking down on rooftop solar would send the wrong message. It could discourage private investment in renewables, slow progress toward clean energy goals, and increase reliance on expensive imported fuels like RLNG. On the other hand, doing nothing could push the national grid into a financial crisis, as fewer users are left to shoulder its rising costs.

A Smarter Way Forward

Pakistan needs balanced reforms to ensure both solar growth and grid stability. Tariff structures should reflect actual costs while protecting vulnerable users. Subsidies or low-interest solar loans can make solar more accessible to lower-income families. The new net-billing arrangements must be managed carefully to ensure long-term viability without discouraging adoption.

The grid must also evolve. With the right upgrades, it can become a flexible and efficient partner in Pakistan’s clean energy future. Battery storage and smart distribution networks can help stabilize supply during peak demand and integrate decentralized solar generation more effectively. About 4.6 GWh of battery storage was imported in 2025, bringing cumulative 2018–2025 imports to 7.6 GWh. Imports are not the same as operating storage, but they show the direction of investment. [114]

Rural electrification presents another major opportunity. In 2024, an estimated 20–30% of the population remained off-grid. [0]note Solar mini-grids and community systems can bring affordable power to these areas faster than traditional grid expansion. Fintech platforms, such as Qist Bazaar, can offer financing solutions for underserved markets.

As solar grows, Pakistan can begin retiring inefficient oil- and diesel-based power plants. The Asian Development Bank’s Energy Transition Mechanism offers a pathway to finance this shift. Local solar manufacturing, installation, and maintenance industries can also create jobs and boost economic growth. [0]note

What’s the Endgame?

Pakistan’s energy future doesn’t have to be a choice between solar and the grid. Instead, the grid must adapt to support this decentralized transition. A smarter, cleaner, and more inclusive energy system is within reach if the right policies are in place.

This is more than an energy revolution. It’s a chance to reshape Pakistan’s economy, reduce inequality, and take a big step toward climate resilience.
