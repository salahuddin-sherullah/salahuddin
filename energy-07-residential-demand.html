Growing Residential Demand

How electric transport, cooking, heating and water heating could put Pakistan’s spare power capacity to productive use.

Pakistan finds itself in a puzzling spot: we have more electricity capacity than we use, yet consumers are finding alternatives to the national grid. Utility-scale installed capacity stood at 41,121 MW in FY25, with served system peaks around 28,000 MW in summer and 16,000 MW in winter. [1][2] note

So why are people opting out?

The Solar Surge

High tariffs are driving people to rooftop solar. Even after the April 2025 reduction, the announced national average tariff was Rs. 37.64 per unit, with a domestic average around Rs. 34.37. [14][90] Pakistan imported 17.9 GW of solar modules in FY25, and cumulative imports reached about 55.7 GW by May 2026. [2][94] For households that can use their own daytime generation, solar isn’t just greener; it offers a way to reduce expensive grid purchases. The economics have changed, however: new users moved to net billing in February 2026, with export compensation around Rs. 10–11 per unit in the cited 2026 reports. [35][106] note

Keeping the Sun’s Power for the Evening

Solar changes the daytime bill; a battery can carry some of that benefit into the evening. By storing surplus generation for later use, households can buy less during expensive hours and obtain backup during outages. Storage has its own cost, however, and the right size depends on when electricity is used. A battery does not make every household independent of the grid. [110]

Renewables First estimates cumulative lithium-ion battery-storage imports at 7.6 GWh over 2018–2025, with about 4.6 GWh arriving in 2025 alone. The distinction matters: GWh measures energy storage, while GW measures the rate at which a battery can deliver power. Import volumes also do not establish how much storage has been installed and is operating. [114]

But what happens when we finally rationalize tariffs? Demand can return. And two big sectors are ready to grow: transportation and household electrification.

Charging Up Pakistan’s Transport Sector

By 2024, Pakistan had over 30 million motorcycles and nearly 6 million cars. Projections at the time put the 2030 fleet at 40 million motorcycles and 6.7 million cars. Transport’s annual imported-fuel bill was estimated at $13 billion and its share of emissions at 30%. [0]note Transport still dominates petroleum demand, accounting for 82.5% of petroleum-product consumption in July–March FY26. [7]

Electrifying transport can connect that rooftop investment to another everyday expense. The NEV Policy 2025–30 targets new energy vehicles at 30% of new vehicle sales by 2030 and plans for 3,000 charging stations. This is a sales target, not a promise that 30% of vehicles already on the road will be electric by then. [115]

For an e-bike rider, the decision comes down to an affordable purchase, reliable charging and the cost of each kilometre. Higher petrol prices can shorten payback, but a single payback period cannot describe every rider. Daily distance, electricity prices, financing and eventual battery replacement all matter. Charging vehicles during solar-rich hours can also turn new transport demand into useful demand for electricity. [110]

If we fully electrify transport, the 2024 scenario suggests 4,500 MW of new demand, with the potential to cut the sector’s imported-fuel bill and emissions substantially. [0]note

How can we get there?

1. Retrofitting Motorcycles

Retrofitting motorcycles into electric vehicles was a relatively affordable option in 2024, costing between Rs. 65,000 and Rs. 130,000 depending on whether a battery is included. This switch can reduce fuel costs by up to 75%. Companies like ezBike had already introduced this service in Pakistan. However, this approach has its limitations: converted bikes often come with fewer features and production capacity is currently low, making it hard to scale quickly. [0]note

2. Retrofitting Cars

Retrofitting cars is technically possible but faced major practical barriers in 2024. Quoted costs were prohibitively high, starting at around Rs. 2.7 million and going much higher depending on the battery and conversion kit. In addition, the lack of local availability, skilled labor, and supporting infrastructure makes this option more challenging at the moment. [0]note

3. New Electric Vehicles (EVs)

New EVs are entering the Pakistani market. In 2024, electric-bike prices ranged from Rs. 200,000 to Rs. 600,000, and EV-car prices ranged from Rs. 5 million to over Rs. 9 million. Companies like BYD and Dewan Motors are paving the way with new electric models. While high upfront costs remain a hurdle, government incentives and a push for local manufacturing could make EVs more accessible soon. The FY27 budget allocates Rs. 8 billion to the EV scheme. [74][99] note

The shift to electric mobility also brings new business opportunities. These include assembling battery packs locally, manufacturing electric motors, and establishing battery swapping stations, an approach ezBike had already implemented by 2024. With the right policy support, these sectors could grow rapidly and create jobs while reducing reliance on fuel imports.

Powering Homes, Efficiently

Residential demand accounted for 50.8% of national electricity use in FY25 (57,455 GWh), well above the historical global benchmark of 28%. In July–March FY26, households used 39,472 GWh, or 47.5% of consumption in the Economic Survey’s chapter table. The shorter period is not comparable with a full-year total. [7][0]note Still, millions of households use gas for cooking, heating and water. The annual cost of that household gas was estimated at $1.3 billion in 2024, highlighting the opportunity to use energy more efficiently. [0]note

What if we go Electric?

1. Cooking

An estimated 17 million households were connected to gas in 2024, many relying on it for cooking. [0]note Transitioning these households to electric stoves, which are up to 75% efficient compared to 45% for gas, offers a more energy-efficient solution. In the 2024 scenario, this shift could generate approximately 2,100 MW of additional electricity demand.

2. Heating

Home heating needs are especially high in Pakistan’s colder northern regions, where usage peaks during the winter months. Shifting from gas-based heating to electric alternatives could also add about 2,100 MW of demand in the 2024 scenario. This transition would reduce reliance on natural gas and provide opportunities for cleaner energy use during the colder season. [0]note

3. Water Heating

Water heating is another major use of household gas. Replacing gas geysers with electric geysers, known for being safer, more space-efficient, and energy-efficient, could contribute another 700 MW to electricity demand in the 2024 scenario. Though electric geysers cost more to run, they come with advantages like longer lifespan and reduced risk of accidents. [0]note

By electrifying cooking, heating, and water heating, the 2024 scenario suggests a total of 4,900 MW of new electricity demand. This shift not only opens doors for increased grid utilization but also supports the transition to cleaner and more efficient household energy use. [0]note

The Bigger Picture

Electrifying just two sectors, transport and households, could add 9,400 MW of demand in the 2024 scenario and cut billions in fuel and gas imports. It would also open up massive markets for: [0]note

  • Electric stoves and cookware
  • EV parts and assembly
  • Battery manufacturing and swapping
  • Motor and power electronics production

But to unlock this potential, tariff rationalization and investment in infrastructure are key. The grid must be ready not just to power homes, but to power change.

Reporting dates, historical figures and qualifications
  • FY25 capacity and served seasonal peaks replace the old 46,000 MW / 30,000 MW / 17,000 MW comparison. The original 23,000 MW average-demand figure is not updated. NEPRA’s computed peak above 33 GW has a different basis. [0][1][2][9]
  • Updated FY26 consumption uses Economic Survey 2025–26, chapter Table 14.3: July–March household use of 39,472 GWh (47.48%) and industrial use of 26,205 GWh (31.52%). The statistical appendix reports different provisional totals, including 40,189 GWh for households and 26,236 GWh for industry. The narrative consistently uses the chapter table, matching the supplied update, and does not combine those two series. [7][108]
  • Solar imports are not operating installed capacity. New-user net billing replaced the old Rs. 22 export-credit / 2–3-year payback illustration. Existing-agreement protections and export compensation are dated policy details, not universal guaranteed rates. IEEFA’s 6.5-year scenario includes a 5 kWh battery with a 10 kW system. [35][97][98][106][109]
  • 2024 edition data, not updated: vehicle counts, 2030 projections, $13 billion transport fuel bill, 30% emissions share, retrofit and EV prices, the 75% fuel-saving claim and company/service examples. They remain estimates published in 2024, not current quotations. [0]
  • The 4,500 MW transport, 4,900 MW household and combined 9,400 MW figures remain historical full-electrification scenarios. Neither a 30% national emissions reduction nor elimination of the entire fuel-import bill is guaranteed: results depend on adoption, charging, the generation mix and replacement energy costs. [0]
  • 2024 edition data, not updated: the 28% global household-electricity benchmark, $1.3 billion household-gas cost, 17 million gas-connected households, appliance efficiencies of 75% versus 45%, and 2,100 / 2,100 / 700 MW household-demand estimates. The Economic Survey’s over 10.9 million gas consumers has a different definition and has not been substituted into the original household scenario. [0][7]
  • Updated storage data: Renewables First’s 2024/2025 sector totals are 1.43/4.59 GWh; its headline rounds 2025 to 4.6 GWh. The 7.6 GWh total is cumulative over 2018–2025. The briefing’s H1 2026 quantity in GW, battery-duty range, charging-station count and vehicle payback estimate are not substituted for dated comparable data. The 30% NEV target concerns new sales; 3,000 charging stations is a 2030 policy objective. [110][114][115]
