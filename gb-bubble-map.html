Gilgit-Baltistan, district by district

Gilgit-Baltistan, the northernmost territory administered by Pakistan, spreads across the ranges where the Karakoram, the Himalaya and the Hindu Kush meet. The boundaries below are its ten districts as of the 2019 geoBoundaries vintage. Four more have since been carved out of them — Roundu, Gupis-Yasin, Darel and Tangir — so the territory counts fourteen today.

At the 2023 census the ten held 1,709,049 people between them, up 14.5% on the 1,492,685 counted in 2017. They are very unevenly filled. Diamer, the most populous, has 337,329; Kharmang, the least, has 61,304 — five and a half times fewer. Growth is just as uneven, running from Hunza at 27.4% over those six years down to Ghanche at 0.8%, which is flat to within rounding.

Each district gets one circle, sitting on the district's centroid and scaled so the circle's area is proportional to its population, not its radius. That is the whole trick of a proportional-symbol map: read the ink, not the width. Scaling the radius instead would make Diamer read as about thirty times Kharmang rather than five and a half.

It opens on Pakistan and zooms in on the ten districts, so the region is placed before it is measured. The opening frame follows Pakistani convention and shows the whole of the former princely state of Jammu & Kashmir within the national boundary, with the Line of Control drawn dashed. The boundary layer alone would not do this: geoBoundaries maps de-facto administration and stops at the Line of Control, so the India-administered portion is added from Natural Earth's disputed-areas layer. Neighbours are labelled for orientation only — no borders are drawn for them.

Hover a circle while the map is showing for its 2017 and 2023 counts and the growth between them — once they line up, each one is labelled outright. Note that the boundaries are the ten pre-2019 districts, so the four units split off since are counted inside their parents.

Notes on the data

The boundaries come from geoBoundaries' open Pakistan ADM2 layer, simplified to a ~400 m tolerance — enough to cut the outlines from 2,347 points to 1,166 without any visible change at this size. For a sense of what that costs: the simplified polygons measure 69,088 km² against the ~73,000 km² usually quoted for the territory. Fine for placing circles, not for measuring land.

The population table is used as supplied, but it is internally consistent: every stated growth rate matches its own two columns, and the 2017 figures sum to 1,492,685 against the 1,492,924 published for the territory that year — a 239-person difference, so this is the published district split rather than a reallocation of it.

Two things were checked rather than assumed. Every circle's centroid was tested to fall inside its own district, and so was every settlement in the companion gb-settlements.json — a first pass put Kharmang's marker in neighbouring Ghanche, which is exactly the kind of error a bubble map hides well. District labels are placed by a small search that will not let a name drift into a neighbouring district.

If you reuse this file, one trap is worth knowing: d3-geo wants the opposite polygon winding from the GeoJSON spec. Hand it spec-compliant counter-clockwise rings and every district is read as "the whole sphere minus this shape" — the map fills solid grey and the projection collapses to a world view. The rings in this file are already flipped, and the check is in a comment beside them.

Nine measures sit behind the boxes under the chart, and the headline figure is not the same kind of number for all of them. Populations, megawatts and tourist counts add up, so their headline is a sum. A poverty index or a literacy rate does not — adding ten percentages together means nothing, and a plain average would weight Hunza's 65,000 people the same as Diamer's 337,000. Those use a population-weighted mean instead, which is why their headline reads "Average" rather than "Total". The difference is not small: literacy is 52.4% weighted against 54.4% unweighted, child labour 14.0% against 16.2%.

On the map the circles are grey; they take on colour as they gather into the row, and give it back if you scroll the sequence in reverse. The colours run by rank within whichever measure is showing — darkest for the highest value, brightest for the lowest. That is redundant encoding: in a sorted row it repeats what the order and the circle sizes already say, rather than carrying a second variable. The value labels stay near-black instead of matching their circles, because the light end of the ramp is not legible as text on white.

The closing figure is a single territory-level estimate with no district breakdown behind it. Rather than size ten circles by numbers that do not exist, the ten gather, give up their colour, fade out, and one circle takes their place. That circle stands for the territory, not for any apportionment: the figure is stated rather than encoded, and it is written into the source as a string rather than summed from the table. Give each district a gdp value and add it to METRICS if a breakdown turns up, and it becomes a tenth selectable measure like the others.

Two gaps in the source table are shown rather than smoothed over. Ghanche has no child-labour figure, so it gets no circle and reads n/a, and that measure's headline covers nine districts, not ten. And the supplied densities do not all reconcile with population divided by area: Hunza and Ghanche match closely, but Kharmang reads 10 per sq.km against 22.6 computed, Shigar 20 against 9.3 and Skardu 27 against 40.6 — most likely because they are based on post-2019 district areas, after the splits. They are used as given.

To put a different measure on it, edit DISTRICT_DATA near the top: one row per district with an m object of measure values, and add a matching entry to METRICS saying how to format it, whether it sums or averages, and where to put the two legend circles. The circles, the row order, the labels, the legend and the headline all follow from that. pop2017 and growth are optional and only feed the tooltip.

Boundaries: geoBoundaries gbOpen PAK ADM2 (2019 vintage, public domain), simplified to ~400 m tolerance.
