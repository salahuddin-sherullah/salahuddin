High electricity prices in Pakistan are impacting households and making industries less competitive. For many, energy bills consume a substantial portion of monthly income, adding to the financial burden on everyday Pakistanis. But why is electricity so expensive, and what can be done to reduce costs? To answer these questions, we break down how the power system operates and where the key issues lie.

{{pdf:01.02}}

## Pakistan’s Power System Structure

{{pdf:01.03}}

Pakistan's power system has three main components: generation, transmission, and distribution. Each stage plays a crucial role in how electricity reaches consumers, and challenges in each contribute to high electricity prices.

{{pdf:01.04}}

1. **Generation**: This is where electricity is produced. The generation sector is overseen by the National Electric Power Regulatory Authority (NEPRA).

{{pdf:01.05}}

2. **Transmission**: Once electricity is generated, it’s transmitted over long distances from power plants to distribution companies (DISCOs). The national network is operated by the National Grid Company (NGC, formerly NTDC); system and market operation now sit with ISMO, under NEPRA’s regulation. [13]

{{pdf:01.06}}

3. **Distribution**: Finally, DISCOs bring power from transmission lines to the meters, delivering it to households, businesses, and industries.

## Generation: The Starting Point of Power

{{pdf:01.08}}

At the end of FY25, Pakistan’s utility-scale installed power capacity stood at 41,121 MW, about 64% above the roughly 25,000 MW installed in 2015. [1][2] However, despite this growth, challenges persist due to over-reliance on imported fuels, underutilization, and varying costs across regions. Including 7,319 MW of net-metered solar, the Economic Survey’s March 2026 total is 49,651 MW. [7] [N]

## Capacity Mix

{{pdf:01.14}}

Pakistan’s energy capacity relies heavily on thermal sources, making it vulnerable to fluctuations in global fuel prices. In FY25, the capacity mix was: [2]

{{pdf:01.15}}

- **Thermal (Oil, Gas, Coal):** About 57% of capacity (23.2 GW), with 18% from coal, 11% from gas, 8% from furnace oil and 19% from RLNG. [2] [N]

{{pdf:01.16}}

- **Hydel (Dams like Tarbela):** About 28% of total capacity (11.5 GW), providing a lower-cost alternative. [2]

{{pdf:01.17}}

- **Nuclear:** About 9% (3.6 GW), with key facilities in Karachi and Chashma. [2]

{{pdf:01.18}}

- **Utility-Scale Renewables and Small Plants:** About 7% (2.8 GW), excluding rooftop solar. [2]

{{chart:thermal}}

The power system also extends beyond these utility-scale plants. A panel on a roof generates electricity where it is used, often outside conventional grid statistics. Ember and Renewables First estimate that distributed solar supplied 28% of electricity in FY25, up from 10% in FY23. These are modelled generation shares, not shares of installed utility capacity. [111]

{{chart:distributed-solar}}

That changes what energy security means. A household producing some of its own power needs less imported fuel behind its electricity supply. But solar output varies through the day, so a growing rooftop fleet makes storage, flexible demand and a functioning grid more valuable. Consumers are gaining more control over their own supply, while the shared network still has to carry them through the hours when local generation falls short. [110]

{{pdf:01.19}}

There are three key challenges in Generation

{{pdf:01.20}}

1. **Overcapacity and Seasonal Demand Fluctuations:** Pakistan’s served system demand peaks at about 28,000 MW in summer but drops to around 16,000 MW in winter, leaving a gap of roughly 25,000 MW against installed utility capacity in cooler months. [2] [N]

{{pdf:01.21}}

2. **Reliance on Imported Fuels:** RLNG, imported coal, furnace oil and electricity imports together supplied about 29% of utility-scale generation in FY25, leaving Pakistan exposed to global fuel prices. [2] [N]

{{pdf:01.22}}

3. **Low Efficiency of Power Plants:** Many plants operate below capacity due to inefficiencies, driving up costs for consumers. Thermal and nuclear plants in the CPPA-G system operated at 38.82% utilisation in FY25. [1] The Guddu example describes conditions in 2024. [0][N]

{{pdf:01.23}}

## Transmission: Bridging Generation and Distribution

{{pdf:01.24}}

Transmission is a significant bottleneck in Pakistan’s power system. While utility-scale generation capacity stood at 41,121 MW in FY25, 64% of 500 kV transformers and 59% of 220 kV transformers were operating above 80% loading. The two main transmission systems, the national grid and K-Electric’s network, struggle to efficiently move power where it’s needed most. [2]

{{pdf:01.25}}

There are three key transmission challenges

{{pdf:01.26}}

1. **Infrastructure Strain:** The 2024 research identified overloaded grids at Gatti, New Multan, Muzaffargarh and Lahore, leading to forced load shedding. The newer network evidence shows the Matiari–Lahore HVDC line at only 35% utilisation in FY25, and the Jamshoro–Rahim Yar Khan corridor limited to 1,800 MW. [2] [N]

{{pdf:01.27}}

2. **Transmission Losses:** In FY23, 2.4% of electricity generated was lost in transmission, translating to a loss of PKR 30.7 billion. [0][N]

{{pdf:01.28}}

3. **Missing Transmission Network:** Access estimates published in 2024 put village electrification at 84% in the NTDC-managed system, compared to K-Electric’s 99.2%. Transmission gaps prevent efficient use of low-cost power from the South, which produces cheaper coal-based electricity, while the North relies more on expensive oil and gas. Furthermore, estimates at the time suggested that only 70–80% of the population was on-grid. [0][N]

## Distribution: Delivering Power to Consumers

{{pdf:01.30}}

The distribution dataset covers ten networks, including K-Electric and excluding TESCO. Together they served about 40.4 million consumer connections in FY25. [11] Demand has historically been concentrated in Punjab and KPK (North), with a 77% share against 23% in Sindh and Balochistan (South) in the 2024 regional comparison. [0][N]

{{chart:customers}}

{{pdf:01.31}}

There are three challenges in Distribution

{{pdf:01.32}}

1. **Losses in Transmission and Distribution (T&D):** Ex-WAPDA DISCO losses were 17.55% in FY25, against NEPRA’s allowed 11.43%. Losses above the targets cost about PKR 265 billion. [11]

{{pdf:01.33}}

2. **Under-Recovery and Collection Issues:** Under-recovery accounted for an additional PKR 132 billion in FY25, bringing the combined shortfall to PKR 397 billion. [11]

{{pdf:01.34}}

3. **Performance Gaps Among DISCOs:** IESCO had the lowest T&D loss rate at 8.61% in FY25, while SEPCO had the highest at 39.18%, followed by QESCO at 38.38% and PESCO at 37.15%. [11]

## How Can Costs Be Reduced?

{{pdf:01.36}}

Addressing Pakistan’s electricity cost challenges requires an overhaul of the existing system to prioritize efficiency, reduce losses, and leverage lower-cost power sources. Key steps include:

{{pdf:01.37}}

1. **Reducing Overcapacity and Optimizing Generation Mix:** A rationalized approach to seasonal generation could minimize idle capacity costs. Additionally, using more local coal, nuclear, and renewable energy could reduce dependence on expensive imported fuels.

{{pdf:01.38}}

2. **Investing in Transmission Infrastructure:** Upgrading the transmission network, especially to connect power plants in the South with demand centers in the North, can lower overall costs.

{{pdf:01.39}}

3. **Improving DISCO Efficiency:** Addressing collection and T&D losses through better management practices and targeted investments can reduce financial losses.

{{pdf:01.40}}

By addressing these challenges, Pakistan can move toward a more affordable and reliable power supply, reducing the economic strain on citizens and enhancing the competitiveness of local industries. The following articles explore each component in more detail.
