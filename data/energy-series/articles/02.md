{{pdf:02.01}}

It is no secret that electricity in Pakistan became sharply more expensive between 2021 and 2024. From 2020 to 2024, per-unit prices rose by more than 20% YoY. That far exceeded the historical increase of less than 8% YoY. Base electricity tariffs doubled in less than five years and, if you include taxes and additional fixed charges, then the price per unit almost tripled. [0][N] Relief arrived in April 2025, when the announced national average tariff fell by Rs. 7.41 to Rs. 37.64 per unit. [14][15] But the question remains: why is electricity so expensive in Pakistan?

{{pdf:02.02}}

First let’s consider how Pakistan compared to other regional economies in 2024. Electricity costs were among the highest in South Asia, a situation that has significant implications for the economy, businesses, and household budgets. With a tariff of $0.21 per kWh, Pakistan’s electricity was nearly three times as expensive as in India ($0.08) and Bangladesh ($0.075). While neighboring countries have seen a decline in electricity prices between 2021 and 2024, Pakistan’s prices surged from $0.13 to $0.21 per kWh, an approximately 62% increase. [0][N]

To understand why electricity got expensive so quickly, we need to break down the cost components that make up an electricity bill.

## 1. Generation Costs

{{pdf:02.07}}

The largest portion of the bill, electricity generation costs, accounted for nearly half (48%) of the final cost in the 2024 illustration and is driven by the following four factors: [0][N]

{{pdf:02.08}}

### 1. Imported Fuel Dependency

{{pdf:02.09}}

Although Pakistan has made progress in reducing its reliance on imported fuel, the sector remains highly exposed to global fuel price volatility. RLNG, imported coal, furnace oil and electricity imports supplied about 29% of utility-scale generation in FY25. [2] Global disruptions can quickly turn this exposure into higher bills. During the Russia–Ukraine shock, oil prices rose by 85%, coal by 200% and natural gas by nearly 300%. [0][N] The Economic Survey 2025–26 describes renewed disruption around the Strait of Hormuz, underscoring the continuing risk of imported energy. [7]

Import dependence is also a question of routes. IEEFA’s March 2026 assessment put Qatar and the UAE at 99% of Pakistan’s LNG imports. Disruption around the Strait of Hormuz can therefore interrupt the supply itself, even when a buyer has a contract and money to pay. [112]

{{chart:lng-exposure}}

When cargoes become scarce, generators must find substitutes, pay more, or produce less. The consequences reach well beyond the power bill: transport and industry face higher costs, while fuel imports compete for foreign exchange. Rooftop solar reduces part of this exposure, but cannot by itself replace fuel used after sunset or in vehicles. [110]

{{chart:imports}}

{{pdf:02.10}}

Furthermore, RLNG (Regasified Liquefied Natural Gas) continues to be a substantial component of Pakistan’s energy mix, supplying 21.4% of utility-scale generation in FY25. [2] Between 2020 and 2023, RLNG prices reached record highs, peaking at $54/MMBTU in August 2022. Even modest price increases can have a significant effect: a 2024 estimate suggested that a 10% rise in RLNG prices would add 2% to the per-unit cost of electricity. [0][N]

{{chart:fuel-cost}}

{{pdf:02.11}}

### 2. Dollar-Denominated Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs)

{{pdf:02.12}}

A large portion of the PPAs Pakistan has with independent power producers (IPPs) is denominated in USD. This means that the devaluation of the Pakistani Rupee (PKR) has a direct impact on electricity costs. Between 2018 and September 2023, the exchange rate moved from PKR 109 per USD to a peak of 306, an approximately 181% rise in the rupee price of a dollar. This depreciation alone increased per-unit electricity costs by roughly 14 rupees between 2021 and 2024. [0][N] CPPA-G’s CY2027 planning assumption is PKR 279 per USD. [20]

{{pdf:02.13}}

### 3. Rising Interest Costs

{{pdf:02.14}}

High interest rates in the US and Pakistan added to generation costs since we pay interest payments on the financing of the power plants. The US Federal Reserve’s rate rose from 0.5% in March 2022 to a peak of 5.5% in July 2023, making debt servicing more expensive. During this period, Pakistan’s own interest rate jumped from 9.75% to 22%, contributing an additional 4.3 rupees per unit to electricity costs. [0][N] Rates subsequently fell, easing financing pressure, although the SBP raised its policy rate to 12%, effective 27 October 2026. [105]

{{pdf:02.15}}

### 4. Excess Capacity Payments

{{pdf:02.16}}

Pakistan’s utility-scale installed capacity stood at 41,121 MW in FY25, but NEPRA’s computed peak demand was only just over 33,000 MW, leaving a gap of about 8,000 MW. [1][9] Capacity charges remain a substantial fixed burden: NEPRA’s FY26 determination put them at Rs. 1.941 trillion, or Rs. 16.67 per unit on that determination’s basis. [31][32] When demand falls short, these costs are spread over fewer units of electricity, increasing the burden on consumers. [N]

{{pdf:02.17}}

## 2. Transmission Costs

{{pdf:02.18}}

While transmission accounts for a smaller part of the bill, approximately 3%, it adds up due to inefficiencies and outdated infrastructure. For example, in FY23, the National Transmission and Despatch Company (NTDC) reported line losses of around 2.4%, translating into financial losses of approximately PKR 30.7 billion. Additionally, limitations in the transmission network mean that only a portion of cheaper energy generated in the south (from sources like the Thar coal plants) can be delivered to the energy-deficient north. In FY25, 64% of 500 kV transformers and 59% of 220 kV transformers were operating above 80% loading, while the Matiari–Lahore HVDC line was only 35% utilised. [2] [N]

{{pdf:02.19}}

## 3. Distribution Costs

{{pdf:02.20}}

Distribution also weighs heavily on electricity costs, accounting for up to 8% of the illustrative 2024 bill, with losses and poor recovery rates leading to higher expenses for paying customers. [0][N] Ex-WAPDA DISCO T&D losses were 17.55% in FY25. SEPCO had the highest loss rate at 39.18%, while IESCO was the most efficient at 8.61%. Bill recovery improved to 96.6%, but under-recovery still cost PKR 132 billion. [11] The 2024 estimates that losses added Rs. 1.15 per unit and full recovery could save Rs. 2.3 per unit illustrate why efficiency matters, though neither estimate has been updated. [0][N]

{{pdf:02.21}}

## 4. Taxes

{{pdf:02.22}}

Electricity consumers bear a heavy tax burden. In 2024, electricity taxes were estimated to generate around PKR 860 billion in revenue, or nearly 9% of the total tax base. Taxes accounted for up to 15% of the illustrative bill. [0][N]

{{pdf:02.23}}

Consumers face several taxes and surcharges, including the standard 18% GST rate. [35] The 2024 tax comparison also included income tax of 10–12% on bills ranging from PKR 500 to 20,000, and a total tax contribution of roughly Rs. 8 per unit. In contrast, agriculture, which uses substantial subsidised energy, contributed only PKR 4 billion in taxes in FY23. [0][N]

{{pdf:02.24}}

## 5. Additional Charges

{{pdf:02.25}}

Additional charges accounted for about 26% of the illustrative 2024 bill and cover several surcharges, from debt servicing to compensating for T&D losses. These include the costs associated with managing the sector’s circular debt, which stood at PKR 1.675 trillion at June 2026. [36] The debt service surcharge is Rs. 3.23 per unit, earmarked for repayment of the 2025 refinancing. [24][25] Fuel price adjustments, based on fluctuations in fuel costs, add further unpredictability and cost. [N]

{{pdf:02.26}}

## The Economic Toll of High Electricity Prices

{{pdf:02.27}}

The high cost of electricity has a profound impact on Pakistan’s economy and its competitive edge in global markets. Expensive power contributed to weaker industrial demand and a 9.3% textile-export drop in mid-2024. A study by the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (PIDE) attributed a 0.75% drop in global competitiveness to high tariffs, estimating a 10% increase in production costs across all sectors. [0][N] Industrial grid sales have since recovered, reaching 29,181 GWh in FY25 and rising about 24% year on year in July–March FY26, as changes to captive-gas pricing brought industry back to the grid. [7][8]

{{pdf:02.28}}

High tariffs drove industries away from grid power towards self-generation. By 2024, there were roughly 1,200 captive power units with a combined capacity around 5,000 MW. [0][N] The trend towards solar energy is also gaining traction among high-paying residential consumers, further eroding the revenue base of grid power suppliers.

{{pdf:02.29}}

## The Path Forward

{{pdf:02.30}}

With the grid struggling to retain high-paying industrial and residential customers, the energy sector is at a critical juncture. If high costs continue driving consumers to alternatives, Pakistan will be left with underutilized, costly capacity that adds no value to the economy.
