Pakistan’s industrial power consumption tells a troubling story. Industry accounted for 25.8% of electricity use in FY25, below the historical global benchmark of 42% and regional comparisons of India (42%), Bangladesh (45%) and China (58%). [7][0][N] Up until 2022, things looked promising, with steady growth. But industrial sales fell in FY23 and again in FY24, as high energy prices eroded competitiveness. There are now signs of recovery: sales rose to 29,181 GWh in FY25, and July–March FY26 consumption increased about 24% year on year to 26,205 GWh, or 31.5% of consumption in the Economic Survey’s chapter table. This covers nine months, not a full year. [7][N]

## The Price of Power

{{pdf:08.03}}

Between 2021 and 2024, industrial electricity tariffs surged by 90%, reaching an estimated average of 16 cents/kWh. [0][N] The April 2025 reduction brought the announced industrial tariff to Rs. 40.51 per unit, while industrialists reported effective rates around Rs. 34–35 in July 2025. [14][101] This steep cost has crushed competitiveness, especially in energy-intensive sectors like textiles. For example, energy costs in spinning jumped from 34% to 54% of total production costs. [0][N]

{{pdf:08.04}}

What’s worse? Industries pay more to subsidize residential consumers, making them less competitive globally and at home.

{{pdf:08.05}}

## The Shift to Captive and Solar

{{pdf:08.06}}

Faced with these prices, many industries went off-grid. Estimates for 2024 put reliance on captive power at over 50%, mostly using gas; textiles controlled 60% of this capacity. [0][N] But the incentives have changed. Instead of the anticipated blanket gas cut-off, captive generation faced a price increase to Rs. 3,500/MMBtu and a new off-grid levy in 2025. This made captive gas less attractive and helped bring industrial demand back to the grid. [75][77][82]

{{pdf:08.07}}

That’s pushed many toward solar. Major players like Gul Ahmed (17.1 MW), Lucky Cement (25 MW), and DG Khan Cement (7 MW) had installed solar plants by 2024. Citi Pharma also cited potential annual savings of Rs. 100 million from solar. [0][N]

{{pdf:08.08}}

Still, this shift has its limits. Use-of-system charges of Rs. 6.23–19.62 per unit for CTBCM open-access users still make transferring solar power between sites expensive, and solar alone can't support 24/7 operations; at best, it powers daytime shifts. [13][69]

The next step is to plan the factory’s whole energy supply. Solar can cover daytime loads; batteries can move some output to later hours; the grid can provide backup and power when either runs short. The value of that combination depends on the production schedule and the cost of reliability, rather than the panel price alone. [110]

Policy is beginning to recognise that flexibility has value. NEPRA’s September 2026 wheeling-auction determination requires participating solar and wind facilities to co-locate battery storage with firm capacity of at least 10% of the generation facility’s firm capacity. This is a rule for the specified auction, not a requirement for every rooftop installation. Firm power capacity and storage duration must both be considered. [118]

For smaller firms, the upfront investment can be the binding constraint. Leasing, shared projects and transparent open-access charges could widen the benefits beyond businesses able to finance their own equipment. Otherwise, the same transition that lowers costs for one manufacturer may leave its smaller competitors paying more for the grid they still need. [110]

## How Do We Boost Industrial Demand?

{{pdf:08.10}}

To revive industrial consumption and competitiveness, Pakistan needs a bold power pricing reset:

{{pdf:08.11}}

### 1. Allow Access to Marginal Cost Power

Let industries use unutilized capacity at marginal cost: what it actually costs to generate extra electricity. This model works in India and Germany, where industrial tariffs are lower and better aligned with production costs.

{{pdf:08.12}}

### 2. Introduce Long-Term Power Contracts

Give industries the option to lock in affordable rates for 10–20 years, similar to Chile’s mining sector, which secures power at 4–6 cents/kWh through long-term deals. This provides price certainty and shields against market shocks. [0][N]

{{pdf:08.13}}

### 3. Enable Direct Contracts with Power Producers

Allow bulk power consumers to negotiate directly with power generators, bypassing distribution companies. The 2024 Chilean comparison put industrial wheeling charges at 1–2 cents/kWh. Pakistan’s new open-access charges are Rs. 6.23–19.62 per unit, replacing the earlier Rs. 27 comparison. [13][69] [N] This approach has fueled Chile’s mining-led export economy. CTBCM’s first 400 MW auction provides a route towards direct trading, though the market remains small relative to the power system. [70][71]

{{pdf:08.14}}

### 4. Ensure Reliable, 24/7 Power Supply

No reform will matter unless electricity is stable and load-shedding free. Frequent outages and merit-order violations disrupt operations and deter investment. [0][N]

{{pdf:08.15}}

### 5. Create Seasonal Incentives

Launch winter power packages and “Use More, Pay Less” schemes to flatten seasonal demand curves. Pakistan’s previous industrial support package led to a jump in consumption from 4.3% to 18% in just seven months (2020–2021). [0][N]

{{pdf:08.16}}

## The Bottom Line

{{pdf:08.17}}

High electricity prices and unreliable supply are choking Pakistan’s industrial growth. By adopting a competitive, transparent, and demand-driven energy policy, modeled on global best practices, we can unlock our unused capacity, fuel exports, and reignite industrial recovery.
