Energy and politics have been deeply intertwined in Pakistan, with political agendas often shaping energy policy in ways that strain the economy and complicate energy access. Policies on fuel, gas, coal, and electricity have long been influenced by political interests, leading to inefficiencies, regional disparities, and market distortions.

{{pdf:06.02}}

The single-buyer model, with CPPA-G purchasing power on behalf of DISCOs, has further entrenched these issues, limiting market competition, delaying reforms, and reducing pricing transparency. Pakistan has now begun a transition to the Competitive Trading Bilateral Contract Market (CTBCM), with commercial operations starting in January 2026 and a first 400 MW wheeling auction initiated later in the year. [13][69][70] This section examines how political decisions impact Pakistan’s energy landscape, creating unique challenges for sustainable development and financial stability.

## Fuel Prices: Politics of Equalization and Subsidies

{{pdf:06.04}}

In Pakistan, petrol and diesel prices are kept uniform across the country despite varying transportation costs, thanks to the Inland Freight Equalization Margin (IFEM). This system averages the transportation cost of fuel nationwide, making it possible for consumers in remote areas to pay the same as those in urban centers. IFEM, estimated in 2024 at roughly 3.6% (around Rs. 10 per litre) of the final fuel price, is managed by the Oil and Gas Regulatory Authority (OGRA) and financed by all consumers, indirectly subsidizing transport costs. [0][N]

{{pdf:06.05}}

The government has also proposed cross-subsidies to control fuel prices, as seen in the 2023 announcement of Rs. 50 per-litre relief for motorcyclists and small-car owners, to be funded by charging the affluent more. Though intended to aid lower-income groups, differentiated prices can increase fuel demand, put pressure on foreign exchange reserves and create opportunities for resale of subsidised fuel. The proposal’s estimated annual cost was Rs. 450 billion, a sum that could fund major infrastructure projects. [0][N] By August 2026, the petroleum levy had reached Rs. 80 per litre, while the FY27 budget proposed ending petroleum subsidies. [72][73]

## Gas Pricing and Supply: Legacy of Mismanagement

{{pdf:06.07}}

Pakistan’s approach to natural gas is rooted in outdated policies that encouraged heavy domestic use at low rates. Discovered in 1952, the Sui gas fields led to a boom in gas connections, particularly for residential use, which grew by an average of 11% annually. Despite dwindling reserves, a 2006 policy encouraged Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) for transport, creating an artificial price gap with petrol and promoting the CNG industry at the expense of long-term sustainability. [0][N]

{{pdf:06.08}}

Gas distribution also stirs political disputes. According to Article 158 of Pakistan’s constitution, provinces with gas reserves (mainly Sindh and Balochistan) have priority in meeting local demands. This provision has led to tensions, especially as Sindh protests the federal government’s diversion of its gas to other provinces. For instance, in 2020 and again in the years leading up to 2024, Sindh raised concerns over gas shortages, arguing that its rights are being compromised. [0][N]

{{pdf:06.09}}

## Subsidies Fueling Inefficiencies

{{pdf:06.10}}

Certain sectors have long benefited from subsidized gas, notably the fertilizer industry, which received discounted feedstock gas to keep urea prices low. However, the Rs. 30 billion subsidy in 2024 did not translate into lower prices for farmers, who face inflated black-market rates due to hoarding and smuggling, often with the alleged involvement of officials. Similarly, export-oriented industries with captive power plants received Rs. 28 billion in gas subsidies in FY23 to boost competitiveness, yet export gains remain limited. In response to IMF pressure, the government has moved from subsidies to penalties: captive-gas prices rose to Rs. 3,500/MMBtu in February 2025, alongside an off-grid levy whose statutory schedule reached 20% in August 2026. [75][77][78] Captive gas use at SNGPL subsequently fell from about 150 to 26 mmcfd as industry returned to the grid. [76][80] [N]

## Coal Supply: Untapped Potential Amid Political Roadblocks

{{pdf:06.12}}

Discovered in 1991, the Thar coalfield in Sindh holds over 175 billion tons of coal, a substantial domestic energy resource. Yet, for decades, development was stalled by revenue-sharing disputes between federal and provincial authorities. It wasn’t until 2015, through Chinese investment under the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), that Thar’s coal was finally tapped. Five Thar coal IPPs, with 3,300 MW of capacity, are now operating. [7][83] [N]

{{pdf:06.13}}

Ironically, while Thar coal lay dormant, Pakistan invested in coal plants that relied on imported coal, just as the world was moving away from coal-fired power. Political hesitations and federal-provincial conflicts delayed Thar’s development, delaying the opportunity to reduce imported-fuel dependence. Conversion and blending programmes are now seeking to use Thar coal at plants built around imports. [7][83]

The attraction of domestic coal is understandable: it reduces dependence on imported fuel and the dollars needed to buy it. Better rail access to Thar can strengthen that option. Yet a locally mined fuel still brings emissions, pollution and long-lived plant obligations. The choice needs to account for those costs alongside the foreign-exchange savings, especially as solar and storage become more competitive. [110]

## Electricity Pricing and the Burden of Cross-Subsidies

{{pdf:06.15}}

To win public favor, the government has historically kept electricity prices low through tariff differential subsidies. This cross-subsidy approach charges industrial consumers higher rates to offset losses from low-income and loss-making residential sectors. However, this policy has incentivized inefficient energy use, while losses and unpaid bills increase the burden on paying consumers. [N]

{{pdf:06.16}}

Since 2020, the government has struggled to maintain these subsidies as rising costs put pressure on public finances. Although Pakistan has abundant hydro potential, an estimated 60,000 MW, developed capacity was only 11,615 MW by March 2026. The 884 MW Suki Kinari project has added to the fleet, but much of the potential remains untapped. [1][7] Major schemes including Mohmand, Dasu and Diamer-Bhasha are still under construction, making timely delivery and management of their costs important to the sector’s future. [84][85][87]

{{pdf:06.17}}

Agriculture also bears heavily on electricity costs, with a reported recovery rate of just 63% in the 2024 comparison. [0][N] Instead of charging higher rates, the government has initiated a program to solarize agricultural tube wells, a move aimed at reducing electricity demand and improving financial viability. Agricultural grid sales fell from around 11 TWh in FY22 to 5.8 TWh in FY25 as solar pumps displaced grid and diesel use. [2] In July–March FY26, agricultural grid consumption was 2,636 GWh, down about 42% from 4,566 GWh in the same months a year earlier. [7]

## Turning the Transition into Investable Projects

Panels on roofs are already changing the system. Public planning now has to connect that private investment with transmission, storage, cleaner transport and access for households that cannot pay upfront. UN-ESCAP support for Pakistan’s Energy Transition Investment Plan was reported in January 2026. [119] An investment plan can help organise projects and financing; it does not itself clear circular debt or guarantee that funding arrives. [110]

The scale of the financing task also needs careful interpretation. The World Bank estimated $348 billion of climate and development investment needs for 2023–2030. Pakistan’s NDC 3.0 sets out $565.7 billion of needs through 2035. These assessments cover broader climate and development priorities, not just electricity, and have different scopes and horizons. They should not be read as the low and high ends of a single power-sector funding estimate. [117][116]

{{chart:climate-target}}

Pakistan’s 2035 ambition is to reduce emissions by 50% against its projected business-as-usual level. Domestic efforts account for 17 percentage points; another 33 depend on international support. The practical test is whether financing terms make grid upgrades, storage and affordable clean energy possible, while protecting consumers from another generation of inflexible obligations. [116][110]

## The Cost of Political Decisions

{{pdf:06.19}}

In Pakistan, the energy sector’s challenges are deeply rooted in political decisions that prioritize short-term gain over sustainable solutions. Fuel and gas subsidies, cross-subsidies in electricity, and mismanagement of resources like Thar coal illustrate how political agendas often sideline economic rationality. For Pakistan to achieve energy security, policymakers must adopt a more sustainable, economically sound approach that aligns with long-term national interests, not just political imperatives.
