Pakistan finds itself in a puzzling spot: we have more electricity capacity than we use, yet consumers are finding alternatives to the national grid. Utility-scale installed capacity stood at 41,121 MW in FY25, with served system peaks around 28,000 MW in summer and 16,000 MW in winter. [1][2] [N]

{{pdf:07.02}}

### So why are people opting out?

{{pdf:07.03}}

## The Solar Surge

{{pdf:07.04}}

High tariffs are driving people to rooftop solar. Even after the April 2025 reduction, the announced national average tariff was Rs. 37.64 per unit, with a domestic average around Rs. 34.37. [14][90] Pakistan imported 17.9 GW of solar modules in FY25, and cumulative imports reached about 55.7 GW by May 2026. [2][94] For households that can use their own daytime generation, solar isn’t just greener; it offers a way to reduce expensive grid purchases. The economics have changed, however: new users moved to net billing in February 2026, with export compensation around Rs. 10–11 per unit in the cited 2026 reports. [35][106] [N]

### Keeping the Sun’s Power for the Evening

Solar changes the daytime bill; a battery can carry some of that benefit into the evening. By storing surplus generation for later use, households can buy less during expensive hours and obtain backup during outages. Storage has its own cost, however, and the right size depends on when electricity is used. A battery does not make every household independent of the grid. [110]

{{chart:battery-imports}}

Renewables First estimates cumulative lithium-ion battery-storage imports at 7.6 GWh over 2018–2025, with about 4.6 GWh arriving in 2025 alone. The distinction matters: GWh measures energy storage, while GW measures the rate at which a battery can deliver power. Import volumes also do not establish how much storage has been installed and is operating. [114]

{{pdf:07.05}}

But what happens when we finally rationalize tariffs? Demand can return. And two big sectors are ready to grow: transportation and household electrification.

{{pdf:07.06}}

## Charging Up Pakistan’s Transport Sector

{{pdf:07.07}}

By 2024, Pakistan had over 30 million motorcycles and nearly 6 million cars. Projections at the time put the 2030 fleet at 40 million motorcycles and 6.7 million cars. Transport’s annual imported-fuel bill was estimated at $13 billion and its share of emissions at 30%. [0][N] Transport still dominates petroleum demand, accounting for 82.5% of petroleum-product consumption in July–March FY26. [7]

Electrifying transport can connect that rooftop investment to another everyday expense. The NEV Policy 2025–30 targets new energy vehicles at 30% of new vehicle sales by 2030 and plans for 3,000 charging stations. This is a sales target, not a promise that 30% of vehicles already on the road will be electric by then. [115]

For an e-bike rider, the decision comes down to an affordable purchase, reliable charging and the cost of each kilometre. Higher petrol prices can shorten payback, but a single payback period cannot describe every rider. Daily distance, electricity prices, financing and eventual battery replacement all matter. Charging vehicles during solar-rich hours can also turn new transport demand into useful demand for electricity. [110]

{{pdf:07.08}}

If we fully electrify transport, the 2024 scenario suggests 4,500 MW of new demand, with the potential to cut the sector’s imported-fuel bill and emissions substantially. [0][N]

### How can we get there?

{{pdf:07.10}}

### 1. Retrofitting Motorcycles

{{pdf:07.11}}

Retrofitting motorcycles into electric vehicles was a relatively affordable option in 2024, costing between Rs. 65,000 and Rs. 130,000 depending on whether a battery is included. This switch can reduce fuel costs by up to 75%. Companies like ezBike had already introduced this service in Pakistan. However, this approach has its limitations: converted bikes often come with fewer features and production capacity is currently low, making it hard to scale quickly. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.12}}

### 2. Retrofitting Cars

{{pdf:07.13}}

Retrofitting cars is technically possible but faced major practical barriers in 2024. Quoted costs were prohibitively high, starting at around Rs. 2.7 million and going much higher depending on the battery and conversion kit. In addition, the lack of local availability, skilled labor, and supporting infrastructure makes this option more challenging at the moment. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.14}}

### 3. New Electric Vehicles (EVs)

{{pdf:07.15}}

New EVs are entering the Pakistani market. In 2024, electric-bike prices ranged from Rs. 200,000 to Rs. 600,000, and EV-car prices ranged from Rs. 5 million to over Rs. 9 million. Companies like BYD and Dewan Motors are paving the way with new electric models. While high upfront costs remain a hurdle, government incentives and a push for local manufacturing could make EVs more accessible soon. The FY27 budget allocates Rs. 8 billion to the EV scheme. [74][99] [N]

{{pdf:07.16}}

The shift to electric mobility also brings new business opportunities. These include assembling battery packs locally, manufacturing electric motors, and establishing battery swapping stations, an approach ezBike had already implemented by 2024. With the right policy support, these sectors could grow rapidly and create jobs while reducing reliance on fuel imports.

## Powering Homes, Efficiently

{{pdf:07.18}}

Residential demand accounted for 50.8% of national electricity use in FY25 (57,455 GWh), well above the historical global benchmark of 28%. In July–March FY26, households used 39,472 GWh, or 47.5% of consumption in the Economic Survey’s chapter table. The shorter period is not comparable with a full-year total. [7][0][N] Still, millions of households use gas for cooking, heating and water. The annual cost of that household gas was estimated at $1.3 billion in 2024, highlighting the opportunity to use energy more efficiently. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.19}}

## What if we go Electric?

{{pdf:07.20}}

### 1. Cooking

{{pdf:07.21}}

An estimated 17 million households were connected to gas in 2024, many relying on it for cooking. [0][N] Transitioning these households to electric stoves, which are up to 75% efficient compared to 45% for gas, offers a more energy-efficient solution. In the 2024 scenario, this shift could generate approximately 2,100 MW of additional electricity demand.

{{pdf:07.22}}

### 2. Heating

{{pdf:07.23}}

Home heating needs are especially high in Pakistan’s colder northern regions, where usage peaks during the winter months. Shifting from gas-based heating to electric alternatives could also add about 2,100 MW of demand in the 2024 scenario. This transition would reduce reliance on natural gas and provide opportunities for cleaner energy use during the colder season. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.24}}

### 3. Water Heating

{{pdf:07.25}}

Water heating is another major use of household gas. Replacing gas geysers with electric geysers, known for being safer, more space-efficient, and energy-efficient, could contribute another 700 MW to electricity demand in the 2024 scenario. Though electric geysers cost more to run, they come with advantages like longer lifespan and reduced risk of accidents. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.26}}

By electrifying cooking, heating, and water heating, the 2024 scenario suggests a total of 4,900 MW of new electricity demand. This shift not only opens doors for increased grid utilization but also supports the transition to cleaner and more efficient household energy use. [0][N]

{{pdf:07.27}}

## The Bigger Picture

{{pdf:07.28}}

Electrifying just two sectors, transport and households, could add 9,400 MW of demand in the 2024 scenario and cut billions in fuel and gas imports. It would also open up massive markets for: [0][N]

{{pdf:07.29}}

- Electric stoves and cookware

{{pdf:07.30}}

- EV parts and assembly

{{pdf:07.31}}

- Battery manufacturing and swapping

{{pdf:07.32}}

- Motor and power electronics production

{{pdf:07.33}}

But to unlock this potential, tariff rationalization and investment in infrastructure are key. The grid must be ready not just to power homes, but to power change.
