In Pakistan, circular debt is a persistent problem that continuously drains the energy sector and the economy. This debt arises when one party in the energy supply chain can’t pay its dues, causing a domino effect of unpaid bills that cycles through the entire system. Circular debt has grown into a massive burden, standing at Rs. 1.675 trillion at the end of June 2026. The stock fell from Rs. 2.393 trillion in June 2024 to Rs. 1.614 trillion in June 2025, before rising again. [2][36][53] [N] Here’s how this cycle works, why it’s so costly, and what steps could help break it.

{{pdf:05.02}}

## How Circular Debt Accumulates

{{pdf:05.03}}

Circular debt is a financial loop where unpaid bills keep passing from one energy sector entity to another. Here’s how the cycle works:

{{pdf:05.04}}

1. **DISCO Losses:** Distribution companies (DISCOs) incur financial losses from high Transmission & Distribution (T&D) losses and under-recoveries, as they can’t collect enough revenue from consumers.

{{pdf:05.05}}

2. **Unpaid Bills to CPPA:** Due to revenue shortfalls, DISCOs can’t fully pay the Central Power Purchasing Agency (CPPA) for the electricity they buy.

{{pdf:05.06}}

3. **Delayed Payments to GENCOs:** With DISCOs paying less, CPPA delays its payments to power generators, including public Generation Companies (GENCOs) and private IPPs.

{{pdf:05.07}}

4. **GENCO Payment Shortfall:** Generators can’t pay fuel suppliers on time, adding another layer to the debt cycle.

{{pdf:05.08}}

5. **Fuel Suppliers Affected:** Fuel suppliers then struggle to meet their obligations to refineries and international fuel providers, spreading the financial strain across the energy sector.

{{pdf:05.09}}

## The Roots of Circular Debt

{{pdf:05.10}}

Circular debt in Pakistan started in 2006 when global fuel prices surged. To shield consumers from skyrocketing energy costs, the government kept electricity tariffs low, leading to a gap between the cost of power generation and consumer prices. Here’s a timeline of how this gap widened: [0][N]

{{pdf:05.11}}

- **2005:** Consumers were charged Rs. 4 per unit while the cost of power was Rs. 4.6. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.12}}

- **2009:** By now, consumers paid Rs. 5.6 per unit, yet the cost had climbed to Rs. 8.5. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.13}}

- **Oil Price Surge:** The global oil price reached record highs in 2008, driving Pakistan’s import bill up by 36%. With power generation heavily reliant on imported furnace oil, costs skyrocketed. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.14}}

Despite some tariff adjustments, the gap between the actual cost of electricity and the consumer price persisted. By 2024, circular debt had grown to unsustainable levels, compounded by several factors. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.15}}

## Major Causes of Circular Debt

{{pdf:05.16}}

Several key issues drive the accumulation of circular debt in Pakistan:

{{pdf:05.17}}

1. **High T&D Losses:** Inefficiencies within DISCOs, such as line losses and electricity theft, cost about Rs. 265 billion in FY25 through losses above allowed targets. [11]

{{pdf:05.18}}

2. **Under-Recoveries:** DISCOs often fail to collect enough billed revenue, resulting in Rs. 132 billion in under-recoveries in FY25. [11]

{{pdf:05.19}}

3. **Interest on Debt:** The debt’s size means interest charges pile up, adding Rs. 58 billion in FY25 and Rs. 14 billion in FY26. Gross additions to circular debt reached Rs. 609 billion in FY26, before offsets. [53]

{{pdf:05.20}}

4. **Capacity Payments:** Payments to power plants for staying operational, even when their power isn’t needed, add heavily to circular debt. HUBCO Coal and Port Qasim, for example, reportedly experienced extended idling alongside capacity claims in 2024. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.21}}

5. **Unfunded Subsidies:** The government provides subsidies to cover the difference between actual electricity costs and the rates consumers pay. However, when subsidies aren’t fully funded, the gap is absorbed as debt. For instance, unfunded tariff differential subsidies added Rs. 135 billion to circular debt between 2019 and 2020. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.22}}

6. **Delayed Tariff Revisions:** The process of revising tariffs and notifying consumers was estimated in 2024 to take about 90 days, causing more debt to accumulate, especially during periods of high fuel price volatility. [0][N]

Fuel procurement can create a second financial trap. Pakistan’s two long-term Qatari LNG contracts cover 6.75 million tonnes a year. When power-sector demand falls, contractual commitments do not necessarily fall with it; redirecting expensive LNG to subsidised gas consumers can deepen gas-sector arrears. [112]

The same system can therefore face surplus contracted fuel in ordinary conditions and a shortage when a shipping route is disrupted. More flexible procurement helps address both risks. Gas-sector arrears and power-sector circular debt must still be tracked separately: they are connected problems, not interchangeable measures of one debt stock. [110][112]

## The Impact of Circular Debt on Pakistan’s Economy

{{pdf:05.24}}

The weight of circular debt affects nearly every sector in Pakistan. As the debt grows, electricity tariffs increase to help cover costs, leading to higher production expenses and impacting profitability. The following 2024 estimates have not been updated. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.25}}

- **Rising Production Costs:** Circular debt-induced tariff hikes raise manufacturing costs by an average of 2.8%. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.26}}

- **Profit Losses:** Firms lose about 1.6% of net profits with every 10% increase in debt-related tariffs. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.27}}

- **Reduced Industrial Output:** At a national level, industrial output drops by 0.09% to 0.12%, which may seem small but has a significant economic impact. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.28}}

- **Export Competitiveness:** Rising electricity costs increase export prices by approximately 3.3%, making Pakistani goods less competitive in foreign markets. [0][N]

## How Pakistan Has Tried to Manage Circular Debt

{{pdf:05.30}}

Over the years, the government has used several measures to try to contain circular debt, but most have been temporary fixes:

{{pdf:05.31}}

1. **Surcharges on Consumers:** The government imposes surcharges to recover debt. The existing Rs. 3.23 per-unit debt service surcharge is earmarked to repay the 2025 bank refinancing over up to six years. [24][62] However, higher tariffs can encourage theft and reduce recovery, making further price increases counterproductive.

{{pdf:05.32}}

2. **Budgetary Support and Liquidity Injections:** The government allocates subsidies in its budget to cover the difference between generation costs and collections. For example, in 2013, Rs. 342 billion was injected to cut the debt, with an additional Rs. 138 billion in 2014. However, these injections provide only short-term relief. [0][N]

{{pdf:05.33}}

3. **Borrowing from Commercial Banks:** To finance shortfalls, the government borrows from banks, creating a debt burden that further compounds interest payments and perpetuates the circular debt cycle. On 24 September 2025, the government signed a Rs. 1.225 trillion financing facility with 18 banks: Rs. 659.6 billion to restructure existing Power Holding Ltd loans and Rs. 565.4 billion in fresh funds to clear producer arrears. [58][62] The reported pricing is KIBOR minus 0.9%, for up to six years. [24][63]

{{pdf:05.34}}

In the FY27 budget, the government allocated Rs. 830 billion to power-sector subsidies, including Rs. 248 billion for the inter-DISCO tariff differential and Rs. 163 billion for K-Electric. [66][108] Yet, without structural reforms, these measures only offer temporary relief. [N]

## Breaking the Cycle of Circular Debt

{{pdf:05.36}}

To address circular debt long-term, Pakistan needs structural reforms that target the root causes:

{{pdf:05.37}}

1. **Reduce T&D Losses and Improve Revenue Collection:** Investing in infrastructure to reduce line losses, implementing smart meters to detect theft, and improving billing systems can help DISCOs close the revenue gap.

{{pdf:05.38}}

2. **Refinance Debt as Sovereign Debt:** As interest rates decrease, the government can refinance circular debt at lower rates by issuing sovereign bonds, thus reducing interest costs.

{{pdf:05.39}}

3. **Align Consumer Tariffs with Generation Costs:** Ensuring that consumer tariffs reflect actual generation costs can help reduce the need for subsidies. Targeted subsidies through social programs like the Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP) can help protect low-income households from higher rates.

{{pdf:05.40}}

4. **Shift to Local and Renewable Energy Sources:** Retiring inefficient, high-cost power plants and prioritizing local and renewable energy sources can reduce the cost of generation, lessening the burden on subsidies and debt.

{{pdf:05.41}}

5. **Restructure Capacity Payments:** Reviewing and renegotiating capacity payments, particularly for idle or inefficient plants, can prevent further additions to circular debt.

{{pdf:05.42}}

Circular debt has become a heavy and self-perpetuating burden on Pakistan’s economy. Addressing this issue requires a shift from temporary financial fixes to structural reforms that reduce inefficiencies, improve cost recovery, and promote renewable energy. By taking these steps, Pakistan can create a more stable, sustainable energy sector that supports economic growth without cycling deeper into debt.
